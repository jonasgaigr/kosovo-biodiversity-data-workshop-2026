Towards Natura 2000

Answers to the Ministry’s questions from the GIS and biodiversity-data sessions of the TAIEX Expert Mission, Pristina, 28–30 September 2026

Published

23 September 2026

The Ministry put a set of questions to the expert team about moving from Kosovo’s present biodiversity data towards a Natura 2000 network. This page answers them as far as they fall within five sessions of the mission: spatial analysis, condition evaluation, GBIF data, field data collection and automated reporting. How sites are proposed, how Article 17 reporting works and how the institutions divide the work are the subject of the sessions by Karel Chobot and Martin Koška, and are not answered here. The numbering follows the Ministry’s list; question 7 lies outside these sessions.

Each answer gives the recommendation first, then what Kosovo’s own data show about it. Those figures are computed from the same GBIF extract, protected-area register and pipeline as the main report, so they move with it.

1. Which data are good enough for which decision

Treat fitness for use as a property of each record, computed by a written rule, in three tiers: national screening, candidate-site delineation and the Standard Data Form (SDF). A record moves up a tier by gaining precision, currency, documentation and validation, never because the decision it is wanted for is more important.

National screening Candidate-site delineation Standard Data Form
Spatial precision Within the screening cell; 10 km atlas records for 10 km screening only Points to 100 m or better; habitat polygons mapped at 1:10,000 As for delineation, and tied to a survey unit: plot, transect, polygon
Currency Any date; records before 2000 flagged as historical Observed within the last 12 years, two six-year reporting cycles From a standardised survey, ideally within the current reporting cycle
Documentation A citable source (dataset DOI or reference); name resolved against a taxonomic backbone + observer, date and method; a photograph or specimen for difficult taxa + survey protocol, effort, and counts with their units
Validation Automated: coordinates, country, outliers, name matching Verified by a named specialist for the taxonomic group + checked at site level, and given an SDF data-quality code: G, M, P or VP
Can support Where to look; gap analysis; survey planning Drawing and justifying a boundary The population, area and assessment fields of the SDF

The values are proposals for national agreement, not established standards. Keep them as rows in a table rather than sentences in a guideline, so that moving a threshold is a one-line change anyone can review, and the tier of every record can be recomputed when it moves.

Two rules hold at every tier. A missing record is not evidence of absence; only surveys that record effort and non-detections can support one. And a name resolved by a backbone can still be the wrong taxon: synonymy in a global backbone can fold a Balkan endemic into a widespread species, or a domestic animal into its wild ancestor. A record whose name changed during matching is one for a specialist to look at.

What Kosovo’s data show

Records All Stated uncertainty ≤ 100 m … and observed since 2014 … and observer named
All records 46,831 3,180 3,045 2,644 (5.6 %)
Birds Directive, Annex I 4,215 213 211 211 (5.0 %)

By these rules, 5.6 % of GBIF records could support a boundary, and even those still need a specialist’s verification. No GBIF record reaches the SDF tier on its own: the extract carries neither a survey protocol nor a measure of effort, and that tier needs both.

The Annex I bird row shows why the tiers matter. 82 % of Kosovo’s Annex I bird records come from eBird, which publishes the checklist’s location without a stated uncertainty. For a stationary count at a wetland that point is close to the birds; for a travelling checklist they may be kilometres away. The rule cannot tell the two apart, so it places both at the screening tier until the effort fields are read from eBird’s own dataset (question 6).

For Natura 2000 purposes GBIF is a screening layer, and a good one: it says where the evidence is and where it is missing. The precision profile in the main report gives the full distribution.

Discussed in: GBIF data (Day 2), “How precisely are the records placed?”; Field data collection (Day 3), “What a form enforces at the point of capture”. A 15 m accuracy constraint on the device makes a new record delineation-grade at the moment it is captured.

2. From the screening grid to a site boundary

The grid says where to look; the boundary comes from the ground. Use the 5 × 5 km cells to rank where evidence concentrates, then discard the cell edges entirely. Draw the candidate boundary from ecological and physical units at 1:10,000, and record the reason for each stretch of it.

  1. Screen. Score the cells from screening-tier evidence: listed features, records, survey effort. Keep the evidence score and the effort count in separate columns, so that a cell that is merely well visited is not mistaken for one that is rich.
  2. Form search areas. Merge adjacent priority cells and buffer them generously. This is an analysis envelope, never a draft boundary; from here on, cell edges play no part.
  3. Assemble the ecological layers inside it. Annex I habitat polygons from field mapping (question 3); the habitat around delineation-grade species records, which means breeding and feeding areas and not just the point; catchments and a terrain model; Copernicus land-cover layers; roads and settlements as barriers.
  4. Draw along those units. Use a habitat edge, a watershed, a forest-stand edge or a river. Snap the line to features visible on the orthophoto or the cadastre at 1:10,000, so that it can be found on the ground and in the land register.
  5. Record why. Give every boundary segment a boundary_basis attribute: habitat edge, watershed, road, cadastral parcel, expert judgement. A boundary whose reasons are written down can be defended; one whose reasons are not will be reopened at every consultation.
  6. Test it automatically. Flag any candidate whose boundary runs along grid lines for more than a small share of its length (10 % is a reasonable start). Flag any long straight segment that follows no mapped feature. A site shaped like its screening cells fails the test by construction.
  7. Check what the line leaves out. Run the site evidence report on the candidate polygon and a 1 km ring around it. Records just outside a boundary are the best guide to what it has cut off.

Keep the grid for what it does well: screening, planning surveys and, later, the distribution maps of Article 17-style reporting. Store every record at its own precision and derive the grid from it, never the reverse. The one exception is an atlas record, whose true precision is the cell.

What Kosovo’s data show

Ligatina e Hencit/Radevës is the country’s richest site for Annex I birds, and its designated polygon covers 1.1 km². Inside it, GBIF holds 2,408 Annex I bird records of 43 species. Within 5 km outside it lie a further 633 records of 34 species, about 21 % of all the Annex I bird records in and around the site. 5 of those species were never recorded inside. The richest locality in that ring, “M9 Bridge over Sitnica River”, lies 2.2 km from the boundary and holds 25 Annex I species.

The birds use more of the landscape than the designated wetland. An SPA boundary copied from the national designation, or from a screening cell, would leave that out. The ring in step 7 is what shows it.

3. Mapping Annex I habitats before assessing them

Several Annex III criteria need both an area and a condition, and only a polygon map carries both. These include representativity, relative surface, and the degree of conservation of structure and function. Points, localities and centroids say where to map first; they cannot be summed into an area. Map in the field at 1:10,000, record condition as attributes scored by a written rule, and validate a random sample independently before any figure goes into an assessment.

Element Recommended minimum Czech practice, for comparison
Base and scale Field mapping on a colour orthophoto, drawn at 1:10,000 (1:5,000 where detail requires) Orthophoto at 1:10,000 or 1:5,000
Minimum mapping unit About 0.25 ha for habitats that normally form large stands; smaller for rare or high-value stands 0.15–0.25 ha recommended for extensive types; smaller only for rarer or high-quality stands
Small and linear habitats Springs, fens, small wetlands and caves as points or lines, with the true area or width recorded Points and lines for occurrences under about 2,500 m² or narrower than 50 m, with true area or width recorded
Mosaics One segment may hold several habitats, each with its share (%) and its own condition Mosaic segments; more than five components referred to the methodologist
Boundary changes Recorded when a boundary moves by more than about 30 m About 30 m
Attributes per habitat Annex I and national code; share of the segment; representativity; degradation; structure and function; typical species present; pressures; surveyor; date; photograph Share and area per habitat; representativity, degradation, structure and function, regional context; surveyor and date per segment
Condition score Derived from the attributes by a published lookup table, not judged afresh for each site A quality lookup table, amended when a new combination occurs
Validation Independent re-survey of a random 5–10 % of segments, reported as agreement on the habitat code and on the condition class —

Czech practice follows the AOPK ČR methodology for updating the national habitat-mapping layer (Lustyk & Guth 2011). The recommended column is written for a first national map; a country mapping for the first time can hold to the same standard from the start and avoid retrofitting it.

Typical species from GBIF

Yes, to decide where to map first, but not for what is reported. The Interpretation Manual of European Union Habitats lists characteristic species for each Annex I type. Where several of them are recorded together in one 1 km cell, that cell is a candidate for early field mapping. The method works where the typical species are conspicuous and well recorded: aquatic and riparian types, and dry grasslands with their orchids and butterflies. It works poorly for forests defined by their dominant trees, and for habitats whose indicators are bryophytes or small sedges. It cannot give an area, a condition or an SDF figure. Co-occurrence is not a diagnosis either: most typical species also occur outside the habitat they characterise.

In Kosovo the plant record limits it further. Plants make up 8 % of GBIF records: 3,740 records of 938 species, of which 1,147 state an uncertainty of 100 m or better. 417 lie in Sharri National Park, which covers more than 500 km². Two or three habitat types chosen for well-recorded indicators are a fair test of the method; a national screen would mostly show where botanists have been.

4. Judging sufficiency without national totals

Do not wait for the denominator; make its uncertainty explicit. Estimate each national total as a range, compute the network’s share at both ends, and record a conclusion for every feature in the categories the EU’s own sufficiency process uses. Where the honest answer is that more research is needed, that process has a category for it.

Interim approach How Suits
The total as a range Lower bound: the mapped or known area or population. Upper bound: modelled potential from land cover, terrain, geology and known records. Compute the network’s share against both. Habitats with partial mapping
Occupied cells, allowing for effort Share of occupied grid cells (the EEA 10 km grid) that fall in the network, counting only cells actually surveyed for that group Species with scattered records
Representation across the range Every main part of the range covered: massif, catchment, altitude belt Every feature, whatever the data
All significant known localities Where a feature is known from only a few sites, include every significant one Rare and priority features
The neighbours’ figures Article 17 estimates of neighbouring Member States and Emerald Network data of neighbouring Bern Convention Parties, as a check on plausibility Transboundary features

The Commission’s working reference values are often quoted as a pass mark. A network holding more than 60 % of a feature’s resource is generally taken as sufficient, and one holding 20 % or less as a priority for scrutiny. They were designed as a pre-selection filter, and the current criteria say they “can never be used in isolation”. With a denominator given as a range, judge rare and priority features on the smaller of the two shares.

The conclusion codes of the EU sufficiency process are an honest way to record an interim result:

Code Meaning
SUF Sufficient: no further sites needed
IN MIN Insufficient, minor: the feature should be added to the SDFs of sites already proposed
IN MOD Insufficient, moderate: one or more further sites or extensions needed
IN MAJ Insufficient, major: no site for the feature has been proposed yet
SR Scientific reserve: further research needed to identify the most appropriate sites
SR Ref List Scientific reserve on the reference list: regular occurrence still to be confirmed
CD Correction of data: the SDF information needs correcting or completing

Keep one row per feature in a sufficiency ledger. Each row records the denominator range and its method, a data-quality code, the network’s share, the conclusion code, its date, and what evidence would change it. That last column turns every SR into a survey commission with a deadline, and makes the interim state as transparent as the final one.

5. Records that survive being shared

This page answers the data side of the question. Every record needs a permanent identifier, a traceable source, a version history and a validation status. Duplicates are linked, not deleted. Sensitivity is handled by generalising the public view, not by hiding the record. Ownership and the arrangement between the institutions are the subject of the joint database-architecture session (Day 1) and Martin Koška’s governance session (Day 2).

Element Recommendation
Identifiers A permanent UUID for every record, survey event and dataset, never re-used, with the provider’s own identifier kept alongside (Darwin Core occurrenceID). A site keeps its code through every boundary version.
Provenance Dataset, source document, observer and method, stored as fields rather than a note.
Duplicates Link, do not delete. Records of the same taxon, date and place from different sources share a cluster identifier; analyses count the cluster once and cite every source.
Versions Every change to a record logged: who, when, what. Every analysis and every SDF submission made from a dated snapshot with its own DOI. Boundaries and SDFs released as numbered versions.
Validation A status on every record, advanced in order: automated checks at import; the taxon specialist for the group; sign-off at site level; approval for SDF use.
Sensitive data One store at full precision. The public view generalises listed sensitive species; named users under a data-use agreement see full precision, and access is logged. The sensitive list has an owner, a review cycle, and species can come off it.
Interoperability Darwin Core for occurrences; INSPIRE Protected sites, Species distribution and Habitats and biotopes for services, through the national geoportal; one taxonomic backbone and shared code lists.

Duplication is already present in the published record. 77 groups of records, 163 records in all, describe the same species on the same day at the same place (to about 100 m) and were published in different datasets. Counted as they stand, they inflate the evidence; deleted, they lose a source. Linked, both counts are right. GBIF performs the same kind of linking across its whole index, and a national system can take its clusters as a starting point.

Discussed in: Spatial analysis (Day 1), “One central Biodiversity Information System” and “NDOP: open by default, precise by permission”.

6. Bird data for Special Protection Areas

Keep one table of bird records and derive the layers from it. Annex I species (Article 4(1)) and regularly occurring migratory species (Article 4(2)) belong in attributes, not in separate datasets that drift apart. To move from IBA, KBA and occurrence data to an SPA proposal, each species that is to qualify the site needs three things. It needs a current population estimate from standardised counts, and a stated criterion that estimate meets. It also needs a boundary drawn around the habitat the population actually uses.

Field Content Why
Annex I Yes or no, from the consolidated Directive The Article 4(1) trigger
Regular migrant Yes or no, from a national list the Ministry adopts and versions The Article 4(2) trigger; the list is a decision, not a lookup
Population type p permanent, r reproducing, c concentration, w wintering: the SDF codes One species can need different sites in different seasons
Breeding evidence Possible, probable or confirmed, as in the European breeding bird atlas A record in the breeding season is not a breeding record
Count and unit A number, with its unit: pairs, individuals, males, calling males Only counts with units can be summed or set against a threshold
Method and effort Protocol, duration, distance, whether all species were reported Turns a record into a survey, and makes absence usable

The working layers are views over that one table: Annex I breeding, Annex I passage and wintering, regular migrants, and congregations. When a record is corrected, every layer follows.

What an SPA proposal needs, beyond the IBA:

  • The IBA inventory is the starting point. The Court of Justice has treated it as the best available scientific reference for judging whether enough SPAs have been classified (Case C-3/96, Commission v Netherlands). An IBA is still not an SPA proposal: its boundary is often coarse, and some of its qualifying species cannot trigger an SPA at all (see below).
  • A population estimate for each trigger species, given as minimum and maximum with a unit, a year and an SDF data-quality code. It should come from standardised surveys over at least two or three seasons:
    • territory mapping or point counts for breeding birds;
    • nest surveys for raptors;
    • International Waterbird Census counts for wintering waterbirds;
    • standardised counts at passage bottlenecks.
  • The criterion it meets. BirdLife’s EU-level criteria are tested definitions: C2, the site regularly holds at least 1 % of a flyway or EU population of a species threatened in the EU; C6, the site is one of the five most important in its region for such a species.
  • A boundary around what the birds use: nesting, feeding and roosting areas, including the fields or river they commute to. Check it against the IBA outline; do not copy it.
  • eBird’s own dataset for analysis. Request the eBird Basic Dataset from the Cornell Lab of Ornithology. It carries the effort fields and whether each checklist reports every species detected, which turns a checklist’s missing species into non-detections. The copy on GBIF is presence-only.

The best bird site in the data is not an IBA

Ligatina e Hencit/Radevës holds 43 of the 70 Annex I bird species recorded in Kosovo. It is designated nationally as a wetland and appears in no IBA inventory. It is therefore the case for the occurrence-data route to an SPA, and it is where standardised counts are most obviously missing. Its birds also differ by season, which is the population-type field at work. 7 of its Annex I species were never recorded there in the breeding season: they are passage or wintering birds, and an SDF would list them as c or w.

8. A pilot, end to end

Yes. Take two contrasting areas through the whole workflow in one field season before applying the method nationally: Sharri National Park for the habitat route and Ligatina e Hencit/Radevës for the bird route. A pilot tests the method, the data model and the division of work at a scale where mistakes are cheap. It also produces the first draft Standard Data Forms along the way.

## 1. Which data are good enough for which decision {#q1}

**Treat fitness for use as a property of each record, computed by a written
rule, in three tiers:** national screening, candidate-site delineation and the
Standard Data Form (SDF). A record moves up a tier by gaining precision,
currency, documentation and validation, never because the decision it is
wanted for is more important.

| | National screening | Candidate-site delineation | Standard Data Form |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Spatial precision** | Within the screening cell; 10 km atlas records for 10 km screening only | Points to 100 m or better; habitat polygons mapped at 1:10,000 | As for delineation, and tied to a survey unit: plot, transect, polygon |
| **Currency** | Any date; records before 2000 flagged as historical | Observed within the last 12 years, two six-year reporting cycles | From a standardised survey, ideally within the current reporting cycle |
| **Documentation** | A citable source (dataset DOI or reference); name resolved against a taxonomic backbone | + observer, date and method; a photograph or specimen for difficult taxa | + survey protocol, effort, and counts with their units |
| **Validation** | Automated: coordinates, country, outliers, name matching | Verified by a named specialist for the taxonomic group | + checked at site level, and given an SDF data-quality code: G, M, P or VP |
| **Can support** | Where to look; gap analysis; survey planning | Drawing and justifying a boundary | The population, area and assessment fields of the SDF |

The values are proposals for national agreement, not established standards.
Keep them as rows in a table rather than sentences in a guideline, so that
moving a threshold is a one-line change anyone can review, and the tier of
every record can be recomputed when it moves.

Two rules hold at every tier. A missing record is not evidence of absence; only
surveys that record effort and non-detections can support one. And a name
resolved by a backbone can still be the wrong taxon: synonymy in a global
backbone can fold a Balkan endemic into a widespread species, or a domestic
animal into its wild ancestor. A record whose name changed during matching is
one for a specialist to look at.

where the evidence is and where it is missing. The

the full distribution.

## 2. From the screening grid to a site boundary {#q2}

**The grid says where to look; the boundary comes from the ground.** Use the
5 × 5 km cells to rank where evidence concentrates, then discard the cell edges
entirely. Draw the candidate boundary from ecological and physical units at
1:10,000, and record the reason for each stretch of it.

1. **Screen.** Score the cells from screening-tier evidence: listed features,
   records, survey effort. Keep the evidence score and the effort count in
   separate columns, so that a cell that is merely well visited is not mistaken
   for one that is rich.
2. **Form search areas.** Merge adjacent priority cells and buffer them
   generously. This is an analysis envelope, never a draft boundary; from here
   on, cell edges play no part.
3. **Assemble the ecological layers inside it.** Annex I habitat polygons from
   field mapping ([question 3](#q3)); the habitat around delineation-grade
   species records, which means breeding and feeding areas and not just the
   point; catchments and a terrain model; Copernicus land-cover layers; roads
   and settlements as barriers.
4. **Draw along those units.** Use a habitat edge, a watershed, a forest-stand
   edge or a river. Snap the line to features visible on the orthophoto or the
   cadastre at 1:10,000, so that it can be found on the ground and in the land
   register.
5. **Record why.** Give every boundary segment a `boundary_basis` attribute:
   habitat edge, watershed, road, cadastral parcel, expert judgement. A
   boundary whose reasons are written down can be defended; one whose reasons
   are not will be reopened at every consultation.
6. **Test it automatically.** Flag any candidate whose boundary runs along grid
   lines for more than a small share of its length (10 % is a reasonable
   start). Flag any long straight segment that follows no mapped feature. A
   site shaped like its screening cells fails the test by construction.
7. **Check what the line leaves out.** Run the site evidence report on the
   candidate polygon and a 1 km ring around it. Records just outside a boundary
   are the best guide to what it has cut off.

Keep the grid for what it does well: screening, planning surveys and, later,
the distribution maps of Article 17-style reporting. Store every record at its
own precision and derive the grid from it, never the reverse. The one exception
is an atlas record, whose true precision is the cell.

## 3. Mapping Annex I habitats before assessing them {#q3}

**Several Annex III criteria need both an area and a condition, and only a
polygon map carries both.** These include representativity, relative surface,
and the degree of conservation of structure and function. Points, localities
and centroids say where to map first; they cannot be summed into an area. Map
in the field at 1:10,000, record condition as attributes scored by a written
rule, and validate a random sample independently before any figure goes into
an assessment.

| Element | Recommended minimum | Czech practice, for comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Base and scale | Field mapping on a colour orthophoto, drawn at 1:10,000 (1:5,000 where detail requires) | Orthophoto at 1:10,000 or 1:5,000 |
| Minimum mapping unit | About 0.25 ha for habitats that normally form large stands; smaller for rare or high-value stands | 0.15–0.25 ha recommended for extensive types; smaller only for rarer or high-quality stands |
| Small and linear habitats | Springs, fens, small wetlands and caves as points or lines, with the true area or width recorded | Points and lines for occurrences under about 2,500 m² or narrower than 50 m, with true area or width recorded |
| Mosaics | One segment may hold several habitats, each with its share (%) and its own condition | Mosaic segments; more than five components referred to the methodologist |
| Boundary changes | Recorded when a boundary moves by more than about 30 m | About 30 m |
| Attributes per habitat | Annex I and national code; share of the segment; representativity; degradation; structure and function; typical species present; pressures; surveyor; date; photograph | Share and area per habitat; representativity, degradation, structure and function, regional context; surveyor and date per segment |
| Condition score | Derived from the attributes by a published lookup table, not judged afresh for each site | A quality lookup table, amended when a new combination occurs |
| Validation | Independent re-survey of a random 5–10 % of segments, reported as agreement on the habitat code and on the condition class | — |

Czech practice follows the AOPK ČR methodology for updating the national
habitat-mapping layer (Lustyk & Guth 2011). The recommended column is written
for a first national map; a country mapping for the first time can hold to the
same standard from the start and avoid retrofitting it.

### Typical species from GBIF

**Yes, to decide where to map first, but not for what is reported.** The
*Interpretation Manual of European Union Habitats* lists characteristic species
for each Annex I type. Where several of them are recorded together in one 1 km
cell, that cell is a candidate for early field mapping. The method works where
the typical species are conspicuous and well recorded: aquatic and riparian
types, and dry grasslands with their orchids and butterflies. It works poorly
for forests defined by their dominant trees, and for habitats whose indicators
are bryophytes or small sedges. It cannot give an area, a condition or an SDF
figure. Co-occurrence is not a diagnosis either: most typical species also
occur outside the habitat they characterise.

## 4. Judging sufficiency without national totals {#q4}

**Do not wait for the denominator; make its uncertainty explicit.** Estimate
each national total as a range, compute the network's share at both ends, and
record a conclusion for every feature in the categories the EU's own
sufficiency process uses. Where the honest answer is that more research is
needed, that process has a category for it.

| Interim approach | How | Suits |
|---|---|---|
| **The total as a range** | Lower bound: the mapped or known area or population. Upper bound: modelled potential from land cover, terrain, geology and known records. Compute the network's share against both. | Habitats with partial mapping |
| **Occupied cells, allowing for effort** | Share of occupied grid cells (the EEA 10 km grid) that fall in the network, counting only cells actually surveyed for that group | Species with scattered records |
| **Representation across the range** | Every main part of the range covered: massif, catchment, altitude belt | Every feature, whatever the data |
| **All significant known localities** | Where a feature is known from only a few sites, include every significant one | Rare and priority features |
| **The neighbours' figures** | Article 17 estimates of neighbouring Member States and Emerald Network data of neighbouring Bern Convention Parties, as a check on plausibility | Transboundary features |

The Commission's working reference values are often quoted as a pass mark. A
network holding more than 60 % of a feature's resource is generally taken as
sufficient, and one holding 20 % or less as a priority for scrutiny. They were
designed as a pre-selection filter, and the current criteria say they "can
never be used in isolation". With a denominator given as a range, judge rare and
priority features on the smaller of the two shares.

The conclusion codes of the EU sufficiency process are an honest way to record
an interim result:

| Code | Meaning |
|---|---|
| SUF | Sufficient: no further sites needed |
| IN MIN | Insufficient, minor: the feature should be added to the SDFs of sites already proposed |
| IN MOD | Insufficient, moderate: one or more further sites or extensions needed |
| IN MAJ | Insufficient, major: no site for the feature has been proposed yet |
| SR | Scientific reserve: further research needed to identify the most appropriate sites |
| SR Ref List | Scientific reserve on the reference list: regular occurrence still to be confirmed |
| CD | Correction of data: the SDF information needs correcting or completing |

Keep one row per feature in a *sufficiency ledger*. Each row records the
denominator range and its method, a data-quality code, the network's share, the
conclusion code, its date, and **what evidence would change it**. That last
column turns every SR into a survey commission with a deadline, and makes the
interim state as transparent as the final one.

## 5. Records that survive being shared {#q5}

**This page answers the data side of the question.** Every record needs a
permanent identifier, a traceable source, a version history and a validation
status. Duplicates are linked, not deleted. Sensitivity is handled by
generalising the public view, not by hiding the record. Ownership and the
arrangement between the institutions are the subject of the joint
database-architecture session (Day 1) and Martin Koška's governance session
(Day 2).

| Element | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| **Identifiers** | A permanent UUID for every record, survey event and dataset, never re-used, with the provider's own identifier kept alongside (Darwin Core `occurrenceID`). A site keeps its code through every boundary version. |
| **Provenance** | Dataset, source document, observer and method, stored as fields rather than a note. |
| **Duplicates** | Link, do not delete. Records of the same taxon, date and place from different sources share a cluster identifier; analyses count the cluster once and cite every source. |
| **Versions** | Every change to a record logged: who, when, what. Every analysis and every SDF submission made from a dated snapshot with its own DOI. Boundaries and SDFs released as numbered versions. |
| **Validation** | A status on every record, advanced in order: automated checks at import; the taxon specialist for the group; sign-off at site level; approval for SDF use. |
| **Sensitive data** | One store at full precision. The public view generalises listed sensitive species; named users under a data-use agreement see full precision, and access is logged. The sensitive list has an owner, a review cycle, and species can come off it. |
| **Interoperability** | Darwin Core for occurrences; INSPIRE *Protected sites*, *Species distribution* and *Habitats and biotopes* for services, through the national geoportal; one taxonomic backbone and shared code lists. |

```{r}
#| label: q5-duplicates

dups <- overall |>
  filter(!is.na(speciesKey), nchar(eventDate) >= 10) |>
  mutate(date = substr(eventDate, 1, 10),
```

Duplication is already present in the published record. **`r n_clusters` groups
of records, `r nrow(dups)` records in all, describe the same species on the
same day at the same place** (to about 100 m) and were published in different
datasets. Counted as they stand, they inflate the evidence; deleted, they lose a
source. Linked, both counts are right. GBIF performs the same kind of linking
across its whole index, and a national system can take its clusters as a
starting point.

## 6. Bird data for Special Protection Areas {#q6}

**Keep one table of bird records and derive the layers from it.** Annex I
species (Article 4(1)) and regularly occurring migratory species (Article 4(2))
belong in attributes, not in separate datasets that drift apart. To move from
IBA, KBA and occurrence data to an SPA proposal, each species that is to
qualify the site needs three things. It needs a current population estimate
from standardised counts, and a stated criterion that estimate meets. It also
needs a boundary drawn around the habitat the population actually uses.

| Field | Content | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Annex I | Yes or no, from the consolidated Directive | The Article 4(1) trigger |
| Regular migrant | Yes or no, from a national list the Ministry adopts and versions | The Article 4(2) trigger; the list is a decision, not a lookup |
| Population type | `p` permanent, `r` reproducing, `c` concentration, `w` wintering: the SDF codes | One species can need different sites in different seasons |
| Breeding evidence | Possible, probable or confirmed, as in the European breeding bird atlas | A record in the breeding season is not a breeding record |
| Count and unit | A number, with its unit: pairs, individuals, males, calling males | Only counts with units can be summed or set against a threshold |
| Method and effort | Protocol, duration, distance, whether all species were reported | Turns a record into a survey, and makes absence usable |

The working layers are views over that one table: Annex I breeding, Annex I
passage and wintering, regular migrants, and congregations. When a record is
corrected, every layer follows.

**What an SPA proposal needs, beyond the IBA:**

- **The IBA inventory is the starting point.** The Court of Justice has treated
  it as the best available scientific reference for judging whether enough SPAs
  have been classified (Case C-3/96, *Commission v Netherlands*). An IBA is
  still not an SPA proposal: its boundary is often coarse, and some of its
  qualifying species cannot trigger an SPA at all (see below).
- **A population estimate for each trigger species**, given as minimum and
  maximum with a unit, a year and an SDF data-quality code. It should come from
  standardised surveys over at least two or three seasons:
  - territory mapping or point counts for breeding birds;
  - nest surveys for raptors;
  - International Waterbird Census counts for wintering waterbirds;
  - standardised counts at passage bottlenecks.
- **The criterion it meets.** BirdLife's EU-level criteria are tested
  definitions: C2, the site regularly holds at least 1 % of a flyway or EU
  population of a species threatened in the EU; C6, the site is one of the five
  most important in its region for such a species.
- **A boundary around what the birds use**: nesting, feeding and roosting areas,
  including the fields or river they commute to. Check it against the IBA
  outline; do not copy it.
- **eBird's own dataset for analysis.** Request the eBird Basic Dataset from the
  Cornell Lab of Ornithology. It carries the effort fields and whether each
  checklist reports every species detected, which turns a checklist's missing
  species into non-detections. The copy on GBIF is presence-only.

The months are a coarse proxy for population type: early migrants arrive in
April, and some wintering birds stay into March. With breeding-evidence codes
on the records, the proxy would not be needed.

## 8. A pilot, end to end {#q8}

**Yes.** Take two contrasting areas through the whole workflow in one field
season before applying the method nationally: **Sharri National Park** for the
habitat route and **Ligatina e Hencit/Radevës** for the bird route. A pilot
tests the method, the data model and the division of work at a scale where
mistakes are cheap. It also produces the first draft Standard Data Forms along
the way.

```{r}
#| label: q8-pilots

site_stats <- lapply(names(pilot_slug), function(k) {
  gpkg <- file.path("outputs/protected_areas", pilot_slug[[k]],
                    paste0(pilot_slug[[k]], ".gpkg"))
  st_drop_geometry(st_read(gpkg, layer = "site_boundary", quiet = TRUE))

names(site_stats) <- names(pilot_slug)

annex_i_species <- function(site) {
  n_distinct(bi_pts$speciesKey[lengths(st_intersects(bi_pts, site)) > 0])
}
sara <- ibas[ibas$code == "RS024", ]

pilot_row <- function(label, sharri, hencit) {

s <- site_stats$sharri; h <- site_stats$hencit
pilots <- rbind(
```

| Step | Sharri National Park | Ligatina e Hencit/Radevës | Produces |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Database QA** | Evidence tiers applied to every record ([question 1](#q1)) | The same | A tiered record set |
| **2. Gap report** | Site report: unrecorded cells, missing groups | Site report, plus the 5 km ring ([question 2](#q2)) | A survey plan |
| **3. Field verification** | Habitat mapping of a representative sector at 1:10,000; Annex II species surveys | Standardised counts in the breeding season, on passage and in winter | Validated field data |
| **4. Delineation** | From habitat polygons and terrain; IBA boundary requested for comparison | From the area the birds use | A candidate polygon with its `boundary_basis` |
| **5. Assessment** | Annex III stage 1: representativity, relative surface, conservation, global | The SPA criteria, species by species | An assessment table |
| **6. Network review** | The site's share of each feature, given as a range ([question 4](#q4)) | The same, for Annex I species and migrants | Rows in the sufficiency ledger |
| **7. Draft SDF** | With data-quality codes on every value | With population types on every species | Two draft SDFs and a lessons log |

**The lessons log is the main product.** It records every step that took
longer than planned, every attribute nobody could fill in, and every rule that
had to change. The national method is revised from it before roll-out, which is
the point of doing two sites first rather than two hundred.

The evidence packs for step 2 can be produced today, from data already in this
repository:

```bash
Rscript site_reports.R --site NP_001 --site SAC_001
```
